In recent years quantum computing has witnessed a rapid increase in popularity, due to new solutions that can 
solve some of the most challenging problems, not only in computer science but also in many other fields~\cite{biondi2021quantum}.
However, a quantum computer with a sufficient amount of qubits is a serious threat to most of the current cryptographic
schemes, in particular asymmetric ones: quantum algoriths, such as Shor's~\cite{DBLP:conf/focs/Shor94}, can break the problems underlying
most of the current public-key cryptosystems in polynomial time, instead of the exponential time required by classical computers.\\
Since 2017 many institutions have started promoting the analysis of quantum-secure cryptosystems, with the most
prominent example being the Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) standardization contest organized by the U.S. National
Institute of Standards and Technologies (NIST). The standardization process started in 2016 and, from the initial 
69 candidates, two standard candidates and four alternatives have emerged; the current standardization-elected
candidate Key Encapsulation Mechanism (KEM) is Crystals-Kyber~\cite{DBLP:journals/iacr/BosDKLLSSS17}, a lattice-based cryptosystem.
To obtain resilience in the event of a potentially disruptive attack on lattice-based cryptosystems, the NIST selected
four other candidates relying on different computationally-hard problems; excluding SIKE, the remaining candidates,
namely BIKE~\cite{BIKE}, Classic McEliece~\cite{ClassicMcEliece} and HQC~\cite{melchor2018hamming}, are all based on 
the hardness of the syndrome decoding problem.

Hamming Quasi-Cyclic (HQC) is a code-based IND-CCA2 secure KEM whose security relies on the syndrome decoding problem for
quasi-cyclic codes. The scheme comprises both a Public Key Encryption scheme (PKE) and a Key Encapsulation Mechanism, 
obtained by applying the HHK Fujisaki-Okamoto transformation on the former. HQC uses a tensor code with a public generator
matrix $\mathbf{G}$ and a random double-circulant code with a systematic parity-check matrix; it is worth noting that the 
code generated by $\mathbf{G}$ is used only for its error-correcting capability, hence it is public and plays no role
in the security proofs.

\subsubsection{Related Work}
Given the sound security record of HQC, much attention has been devoted to its side-channel resistance. Most of the 
independent analyses focus on extracting electromagnetic and power traces~\cite{DBLP:conf/cardis/SchambergerRSW20, DBLP:conf/pqcrypto/SchambergerHRWS22, DBLP:conf/pqcrypto/GoyLG22},
whilst at the end of Round 2 of the NIST contest the authors in~\cite{DBLP:journals/amco/Wafo-TapaBBGM22} and~\cite{DBLP:conf/sacrypt/PaivaT19}
proposed a timing attack that takes advantage of the non constant-time implementation of the BCH decoder and the dependence
between the decoding time and the amount of errors to correct; these attacks are no longer relevant starting from Round 3, 
as the authors of HQC swapped the BCH code with a Reed-Muller Reed-Solomon code, implementing a constant-time decoder. Nonetheless,
in~\cite{DBLP:journals/tches/GuoHJLNS22}, the authors propose a new timing attack based on a weakness in the \emph{rejection sampling} procedure
employed to generate random sparse polynomials; as a countermeasure, a more careful implementation of the rejection sampling
step has been proposed in the Round 4 submission, fixing the issue.\\
We also note that most of the literature on side-channel attacks on HQC aims at key retrieval, while it is
also possible to carry out a message-recovery attack if the operations in the encryption algorithm, such as polynomial
multiplication, are not constant-time; the requirement of a constant-time execution is further strenghtened by the fact 
that the encryption primitive is used as a subroutine in the KEM scheme.

To date, no dedicated constant-time implementation of HQC targeting embedded systems hass been proposed, despite the 
fact that timing attacks are easier to carry out on these platforms. The current state of software implementations of HQC
tailored for the ARM Cortex-M4 microcontroller, chosen by the NIST as the reference platform for microcontroller benchmarks,
includes only the one in PQM4~\cite{PQM4}, a third-party public software project that collects the implementations of the NIST
Round 4 candidates tailored for Cortex M4. The latest version of HQC in PQM4 is directly derived from the Round 3 reference 
implementation, with no optimizations: as a consequence, the implementation is not constant-time and vulnerable to the timing
side-channel attack presented in~\cite{DBLP:journals/tches/GuoHJLNS22}.

\subsubsection{Our Contribution}
In this thesis we present the first fully constant-time software implementation of HQC, together with a comparative analysis
constant-time binary polynomial multiplication techniques, tailored for Cortex-M4 microprocessors. We provide an experimental
validation of the constant time properties via a battery of statistical t-tests, while varying the CPU frequency to highlight
the stability of our solution across different memory access latencies. Our results show that the optimal choice for constant-time
multiplications on the Cortex-M4 is a FFT-based approach, in contrast with classic divide-et-impera techniques (\textit{i.e.} Karatsuba)
which provide a better performance on x86 architectures.\\
Moreover, to counter the power attacks, we propose a masking scheme that can be applied to the entire cryptosystem, 
and present our findings on its potential efficacy; although our empirical results are inconclusive, they offer valuable insights
into our testing methodologies and pave the way for future research on the topic.
