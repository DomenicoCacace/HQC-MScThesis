Let $a(x), b(x) \in \mathcal{R}$ be two polynomials. To calculate the product $c(x) = a(x)b(x)$ the most straightforward
approach, also known as the \emph{schoolbook} algorithm, requires us to multiply each coefficient of the first with each polynomial of the second 
and add together the results of the same degree. With this method we can calculate the product of the polynomials with $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ multiplications
and $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ additions, as the degree of the operands is at most $n-1$. Even though this approach is simple to understand and implement, 
it does not scale well as the degree of the operands increases: for this reason, other multiplication algorithms have been proposed.

As shown by Bernstein in~\cite{bernstein2001multidigit}, we can think of most of the polynomial multiplication algorithms in the literature
as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifting} the operands to a different ring via a homomorphism $f$
    \item Multiplying the lifted polynomials in the new ring
    \item \textbf{Mapping} the result back to the original ring via the inverse homomorphism $f^{-1}$
\end{itemize} 
By carefully choosing the destination ring and the homomorphism, we can obtain algorithms with drastically better asymptotic complexity:
in the remainder of this section we will discuss two of the most common algorithms, Karatsuba and the Fast Fourier Transform. 

\subsection{Karatsuba multiplication}
The Karatsuba algorithm~\cite{karatsuba1962multiplication} is a divide and conquer algorithm that splits a $n$-degree polynomial into 
three $n/2$-degree polynomials and then calculates the product of the three polynomials in $\mathcal{O}(n^{\log_2 3})$ operations.

Starting from $a(x)$, $b(x)$ of degree $n$ and an integer $m < n$, we can write
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
    a(x) &= a_0(x) + x^m a_1(x) \\
    b(x) &= b_0(x) + x^m b_1(x)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
where $a_0(x)$ and $b_0(x)$ have degree less than $m$. At this point, we can calculate the product $c(x) = a(x)\cdot b(x)$ as
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
        c(x) &= a(x)b(x) \\
             &= (a_0(x) + x^m a_1(x))(b_0(x) + x^m b_1(x)) \\
             &= a_1(x)b_1(x)x^{2m} + (a_1(x)b_0(x) + a_0(x)b_1(x))x^m + a_0(x)b_0(x)\\
             &= z_2(x)x^{2m} + z_1(x)x^m + z_0(x)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
where
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
        z_2(x) &= a_1(x)b_1(x) \\
        z_1(x) &= a_1(x)b_0(x) + a_0(x)b_1(x) \\
        z_0(x) &= a_0(x)b_0(x)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Karatsuba observed that, at the cost of some few additions, we can calculate $a(x)b(x)$ with three multiplications, as we can rewrite $z_1$ as
\begin{equation*}
    z_1(x) = (a_0(x) + a_1(x))(b_0(x) + b_1(x)) - z_0(x) - z_2(x)
\end{equation*}
\begin{example}
From the ring of binary polynomials $\mathcal{R}$ we take $a(x) = 1 + x + x^3$ and $b(x) = x + x^2$; by taking $m=2$, we rewrite:
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
        a(x) &= (1+x) + x^2(x)\\
        b(x) &= (x) + x^2(1)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
and calculate the subproducts as
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
        z_2(x) &= (1+x)(x) = x + x^2 \\
        z_0(x) &= (x)(1) = x \\
        z_1(x) &= (1+x + x)(x + 1) - (x) - (x+x^2) = 1 + x + x^2
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
From the above, we can calculate the full product as
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
        c(x) &= (x)x^{2\cdot 2} + (1 + x + x^2)x^2 + (x+x^2) \\
             &= x^5 + x^4 + x^3 + x
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
\end{example}

The input polynomials can be split into an arbitrary amount of parts, and the product calculated in a similar fashion:
such a product is calculated by the Toom-Cook algorithm~\cite{cook1969minimum}, which is a generalization of Karatsuba's algorithm.

\subsection{Fast Fourier Transform}

We can approach polynomial multiplication in a totally different way: instead of multiplying the polynomial coefficients, we can
evaluate the inputs at a set of points (\emph{sampling}), calculate the pointwise product of the evaluations and then interpolate the points
to obtain the product of the polynomials.
\begin{example}
    Let $a(x) = x^2 + 2x + 3$ and $b(x) = 4x + 5$ be polynomials with real coefficients of degree at most $n=2$; since their product
    will have degree at most $2n=4$ we evaluate the polynomials at $2n+1$ points:
    \begin{align*}
        a(0) = &1\cdot 0^2 + 2\cdot 0 + 3 = 3 \quad &b(0) = &0\cdot 0^2 + 4\cdot 0 + 5 = 5 \\
        a(1) = &1\cdot 1^2 + 2\cdot 1 + 3 = 6 \quad &b(1) = &0\cdot 1^2 + 4\cdot 1 + 5 = 9 \\
        a(2) = &1\cdot 2^2 + 2\cdot 2 + 3 = 11 \quad &b(2) = &0\cdot 2^2 + 4\cdot 2 + 5 = 13 \\
        a(3) = &1\cdot 3^2 + 2\cdot 3 + 3 = 18 \quad &b(3) = &0\cdot 3^2 + 4\cdot 3 + 5 = 17 \\
        a(4) = &1\cdot 4^2 + 2\cdot 4 + 3 = 27 \quad &b(4) = &0\cdot 4^2 + 4\cdot 4 + 5 = 21 \\
        %a(5) = &1\cdot 5^2 + 2\cdot 5 + 3 = 38 \quad &b(5) = &0\cdot 5^2 + 4\cdot 5 + 5 = 25 \\
    \end{align*}
    We can then calulate the pointwise multiplication of the samples
    \begin{align*}
        c(0) = &a(0)\cdot b(0) = 3\cdot 5 = 15 \\
        c(1) = &a(1)\cdot b(1) = 6\cdot 9 = 54 \\
        c(2) = &a(2)\cdot b(2) = 11\cdot 13 = 143 \\
        c(3) = &a(3)\cdot b(3) = 18\cdot 17 = 306 \\
        c(4) = &a(4)\cdot b(4) = 27\cdot 21 = 567 \\
        %c(5) = &a(5)\cdot b(5) = 38\cdot 25 = 950 \\
    \end{align*}
    Finally, we can interpolate the result to obtain the coefficients of the product:
    \begin{equation*}
        c(x) = 4x^3 + 13x^2 + 22x + 15
    \end{equation*}
\end{example}
This approach might appear slower than the schoolbook method: the sampling requires $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ operations, the pointwise product
$\mathcal{O}(n)$ and the interpolation up to $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ if we use a naive method such as Gaussian elimination; however, 
with the right choice of sampling points, we can drastically reduce the operations required in all three steps.

Instead of sampling at $2n$ random points, we can choose the points to be the $N$-th roots of unity, \textit{i.e.} we calculate the Discrete
Fourier Transform of the input polynomials:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{N} x_n e^{-\frac{i2\pi}{N}kn}
\end{equation}\label{DFT}
At this point we can perform the pointwise product in the \textit{transformed} domain and, since $\mathcal{F}$ is invertible, apply the inverse transform:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}^{-1}(X) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N} X_n e^{i\frac{2\pi}{N}kn}
\end{equation}
The algorithms that compute the discrete Fourier Transform of a sequence (or its inverse) are called fast Fourier transforms (FFT), and have an asymptotic complexity
of $\mathcal{O}(n\log n)$.

\subsubsection{Cooley-Tukey FFT}
The Cooley-Tukey FFT~\cite{cooley1965algorithm} is the most common FFT algorithm. It is a \textit{divide et impera} algorithm, which recursively expresses a DFT of
size $N = N_1 N_2$ into $N_1$ smaller DFTs of size $N_2$ each. The simplest form of the Cooley-Tukey algorithm is the radix-2 decimation-in-time FFT, which computes the DFTs of
the even and odd indexed inputs separately and then combines the results. Assuming that N is a power of 2, we can rewrite the DFT as:
\begin{align*}
    X_k & = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N}nk} \\
        & = \sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1}x_{2m}e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N}(2m)k} + \sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1}x_{2m+1}e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N}(2m+1)k} \\
        & = \sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1}x_{2m}e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N/2}mk} + e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N}k}\sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1}x_{2m+1}e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N/2}mk} \\
        & = E_k + e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N}k}O_k \qquad \text{ for } k = 0, \cdots, N/2-1
\end{align*}
where we split the DFT into two smaller DFTs, on the even and odd indexes respectively. Because of the periodicity of the complex exponential, we can also write:
\begin{align*}
    X_{k+N/2} & = \sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1}x_{2m}e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N/2}m(k+N/2)} + e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N}(k+N/2)}\sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1}x_{2m+1}e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N/2}m(k+N/2)} \\
        & = \sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1}x_{2m}e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N/2}mk}e^{-2\pi mi} + e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N}k}e^{-\pi i}\sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1}x_{2m+1}e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N/2}mk}e^{-2\pi mi} \\
        & = \sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1}x_{2m}e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N/2}mk} - e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N}k}\sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1}x_{2m+1}e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N/2}mk} \\
        & = E_k - e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N}k}O_k \qquad \text{ for } k = 0, \cdots, N/2-1
\end{align*}
This means that we can express a DFT of size $N$ as two DFTs of size $N/2$, where we can re-use intermediate results to calculate multiple outputs. 
Since the base case of the recursion has complexity $\mathcal{O}(n)$ we get the recurrence relation $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \mathcal{O}(n)$;
following the Master theorem~\cite{bentley1980general}, the computational complexity of the Cooley-Tukey FFT is $\mathcal{O}(n\log n)$.


\subsection{Additive FFT}
    The Cooley-Tukey FFT works when the $n$ points to sample are $n$-th roots of unity, and hence form a cyclic multiplicative group;
    this and similar algorithms are also referred to as \textit{multiplicative} FFTs, as they are based on the factorization of $x^n-1$.\\
    Unfortunately, multiplicative FFTs do not work when the underlying field does not admit primitive roots of unity, as it happens for fields
    of characteristic two; for this reason, \textit{additive} FFTs were invented~\cite{DBLP:journals/jsac/WangZ88,DBLP:journals/jct/Cantor89}.
    Instead of using the $n$-th roots of unity, additive FFTs evaluate the polynomials at the powers of a primitive $(n-1)$-th root of unity,
    as well as the zero element. Some examples of such algorithms that work on binary fields are the Gao-Mateer FFT~\cite{DBLP:journals/tit/GaoM10} 
    and the Lin-Chung-Han FFT~\cite{DBLP:conf/focs/LinCH14}.\\
    We will now focus on the LCH FFT, as it will be the foundation for a faster approach based on the Frobenius map; for this reason, we will
    consider the case $n=2^m$ where $m$ is a power of 2.


    \subsubsection{Cantor basis}
    In~\cite{DBLP:journals/jct/Cantor89} Cantor showed how to get a special basis in which it is possible to compute the FFT efficiently
    for any $m = p^n$, with $p$ a prime number; later in~\cite{DBLP:journals/tit/GaoM10} Gao and Mateer used a simpler construction for the special case
    $p=2$, where the Cantor basis $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, \cdots, v_{m})$ of $\mathds{F}_2^m$ follows the structure:
    \begin{equation}
        v_1 = 1, \quad v_i^2+v_i = v_{i-1} \text{ for } i > 1
    \end{equation}
    With respect to the Cantor basis $\mathbf{v}$, we define its sequence of subspaces as:
    \begin{equation}
        W_0 := \{0\}, \quad W_i := span\{v_0, \cdots, v_{i-1}\} \text{ for } i \geqslant 1
    \end{equation}

    By construction, we have that $W_0 \subset W_1 \subset \cdots \subset W_{m-1} = \mathds{F}_{2^m}$ 
    and the basis for a smaller space is contained into the basis of a larger space: this means that 
    we can arbitrarily treat elements of smaller fields as elements in the larger fields by padding zeros to the extra dimensions.

    With respect to each element of the basis $v_i$ we can define its subspace polynomial $s_i(x)$, also called vanishing polynomial, as:
    \begin{equation}
        s_i(x) = \prod_{a \in W_i} (x - a)
        \label{eq:vanishing_polynomial}
    \end{equation}
    Since $\dim(W_i) = i$ we have $\deg(s_i(x)) = |W_i| = 2^i$.
    Such polynomials, as shown in~\cite{DBLP:journals/jct/Cantor89,DBLP:journals/tit/GaoM10}, satisfy the following useful properties:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item linearity: $s_i(x+y) = s_i(x) + s_i(y)$
        \item recursivity: $s_{i+j}(x) = s_i(s_j(x))$ or, equivalently, $s_{i+1}(x) = s_i(x)^2+s_i(x)$
        \item $s_i(v_i) = 1$, for $v_i$ in the Cantor basis
        \item the roots of $s_i(x)$ are the elements of $W_i$
    \end{itemize}
    Note that the $W_i$s are also fields whenever $i$ is a power of two; in that case, the vanishing polynomial has the form $s_i(x) = x^{2^i}+x$.

    \begin{example}[Cantor basis in $\mathds{F}_{16}$]
        Recalling that $GF(16) = GF(2)[X]/\langle X^4+X+1 \rangle$ and that $v_1 = 1$, we can compute $v_i = \beta(1) + \gamma(x) + \delta(x^2) + \epsilon(x^3)$,
        where the coefficients can be found by solving:
        \begin{equation*}
            \beta(1+1^2)+\gamma(x+x^2) + \delta(x^2+(x^2)^2) + \epsilon(x^3+(x^3)^2) = v_{i-1}
        \end{equation*}
        The resulting Cantor basis is $\mathbf{v} = (1, \alpha+\alpha^2, \alpha, \alpha+\alpha^3)$. As a consequence, the subspaces $W_i$ are:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item $W_0 = \{0\}$
            \item $W_1 = \{0, 1\}$
            \item $W_2 = \{0, 1, \alpha+\alpha^2, 1+\alpha+\alpha^2\}$
            \item $W_3 = \{0, 1, \alpha+\alpha^2, 1+\alpha+\alpha^2, \alpha, 1+\alpha, \alpha^2, 1+\alpha^2\}$
            \item $W_4 = \{0, 1, \alpha+\alpha^2, 1+\alpha+\alpha^2, \alpha, 1+\alpha, \alpha^2, 1+\alpha^2, \alpha+\alpha^3, 1+\alpha+\alpha^3, \alpha^2+\alpha^3, 1+\alpha^2+\alpha^3, \alpha^3, 1+\alpha^3, \alpha+\alpha^2+\alpha^3, 1+\alpha+\alpha^2+\alpha^3\} = \mathds{F}_{16}$
        \end{itemize}
    Consequently, the vanishing polynomials are:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $s_0(x) = x$
        \item $s_1(x) = x+x^2$
        \item $s_2(x) = x^4+x$
        \item $s_3(x) = x^8+x^4+x^2+x$
        \item $s_4(x) = x^{16}+x$
    \end{itemize}

    \end{example}

    \paragraph{A \textit{novel} polynomial basis}
    In order to perform the LCH FFT, the input polynomial has to be represented in a different basis. Given the Cantor basis $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, \cdots, v_m)$, we define the 
    \emph{novelpoly} basis with respect to $\mathbf{v}$ as $(X_1, \cdots, X_{n-1})$, defined as follows:
    \begin{equation}
        X_k(x) = \prod(s_i(x))^{b_i} \qquad \text{ where } k = \sum_{i=0}^{\log_2 n} b_i 2^i
    \end{equation}
    In other words, $X_k(x)$ is the product of all the $i$-th vanishing polynomials, where the $i$s are the set bits of $k$ in its binary representation.

    \begin{algorithm2e}
        \caption{Basis conversion from monomial to \emph{novelpoly}}
        \label{alg:basiscvt}
        \KwIn{$f(x) = f_0 + f_1x+f_2x^2+\cdots + f_{n-1}x^{n-1}  \in \mathds{F}_2[x]_{<n}$}
        \KwOut{$g(X) = g_0 + g_1X_1(x) + g_2X_2(x) + \cdots + g_{n-1}X_{n-1}(x) \in \mathds{F}_2[x]_{<n}$}
        \KwData{$y = x^{2^i}+x = s_i(x)$}
        \If{$\deg f(x) \leqslant 1$} {
            \KwRet{$g(X) \gets f_0 + f_1X_1$}\;
        }
        $i \gets \max(2^k)$ s.t. $\deg(s_i(x)) \leqslant \deg(f(x))$\;
        $h(x,y) = \hat{h}(y) \gets h_0(x) + h_1(x)y + \cdots + h_{m-1}(x)y^{m-1}$\tcp*{$h_i(x) \in \mathds{F}_2[x]$}
        $h^{\prime}(Y) = q_0(x) + \cdots + q_{m-1}X_{(m-1)2^k} \gets \text{\textsf{BasisCvt}}(\hat{h}(y))$\;
        \For{$j=0$ to $m-1$} {
            $g_j(X) \gets \text{\textsf{BasisCvt}}(q_j(x))$\;
        }
        \KwRet{$g(X) = g_0 + g_1(X)X_{2^k} + \cdots + g_{n-1}(X)X_{(m-1)2^k}$}\;
    \end{algorithm2e}

    \subsubsection{Divide et impera: the Butterflies}
    The general idea of the LCH FFT to evaluate $f(x)$ at all points of $W_k$ is to split $f$ into two half sized polynomials, as well
    as splitting the set of points to be evaluated into $W_{k-1}$ and $W_k\setminus W_{k-1} = \{x + v_k | x \in W_{k-1}\}$; the full algorithm
    is detailed in Algorithm~\ref{alg:lchfft}.

    \begin{algorithm2e}[H]
    \caption{Lin-Chung-Han FFT}
    \label{alg:lchfft}
    \KwIn{$f(x) = g(X) = g_0 + g_1X_1(x) + \cdots + g_{n-1}X_{n-1}(x) \in \mathds{F}_{2^m}[x]_{<n}$,\newline
            $\alpha \in \mathds{F}_{2^m}$}
    \KwOut{$S = \{f(x) | x \in W_m\}$}
    \If{$\deg(f(x))$}{
        \KwRet{$[g_0]$}\;
    }
    $k \gets \max(i) \text{ s.t. } 2^i \leqslant \deg(f(x))$\;
    $g(X) \gets p_0(X) + X_{2^k}\cdot p_1(X)$\;
    $h_0(X) \gets p_0(X) + s_k(\alpha)\cdot p_1(X)$\;\label{line:h0}
    $h_1(X) \gets h_0(X) + s_k(v_k)\cdot p_1(X)$\;\label{line:h1}
    \KwRet{$[FFT(h_0(X), \alpha), FFT(h_1(X), v_{k}+\alpha)]$}\;
    \end{algorithm2e}
    The \textit{actual} computation, performed at lines~\ref{line:h0} and~\ref{line:h1}, is commonly called \emph{butterfly} and
    is schematized in Figure~\ref{fig:butterfly}. Notice that the butterfly performs two field additions but only one field multiplication,
    since $s_k(v_k) = 1$ in Cantor basis.

    \subsubsection{Frobenius Additive FFT}
    In~\cite{DBLP:conf/issac/HoevenL17} the authors showed that, by using the Frobenius map, it is possible
    to derive more values of polynomials from less evaluation points: given $f(x) \in \mathds{F}_2[x]_{<n}$,
    it is possible to prove that
    \begin{equation}
        f(\phi_2(a)) = \phi_2(f(a))
    \end{equation}
    meaning that we can derive the value of $f$ at $\phi_2(a)$ by applying the Frobenius map to $f(a)$.
    Our goal now is, given a polynomial $f(x) \in \mathds{F}_2[x]_{<n}$, to design a set $\Sigma \subset \mathds{F}_{2^m}$
    for $m$ a power of 2 such that we can derive $n$ values of $f$ from $\Sigma$.\
    \paragraph{Frobenius partition}
        Consider the Frobenius map in $\mathds{F}_{2^m}$; with respect to the Cantor basis, it has been shown in~\cite{DBLP:conf/issac/LiCKCY18}
        that the order of the Frobenius map is
        \begin{equation}
            Ord_{\phi_2}(v_i) = 2^{\lfloor \log_2 i \rfloor+1} 
        \end{equation}
        meaning that the maximum order for $v_i \in \{v_{m/2}, \cdots, v_{m-1}\}$ is $m$. 
        If we define $l_m = \log_2 m$, $l_n = \log_2 n$ and $l = l_n-l_m$, we can design a set with maximum order as:
        \begin{equation}
            \Sigma := v_{l+m/2}+W_l = \{v_{l+m/2} + x \mid x \in W_l\}
            \label{eq:frobenius_partition}
        \end{equation}
        with size $n^\prime = |\Sigma| = |W_l| = 2^l = n/m$ and order $m$. By applying the Frobenius map to $\Sigma$ repeatedly, it is possible to prove that
        \begin{equation}
            \Omega = \Sigma \cup \phi_2(\Sigma) \cup \cdots \cup \phi_2^{m-1}(\Sigma)
        \end{equation}
        with $|\Omega| = n$ and $\Sigma$ is a Frobenius partition of $\Omega$~\cite{DBLP:conf/issac/LiCKCY18}.

    \paragraph{Polynomial evaluation}
        Now that we built the Frobenius partition, we have to evaluate $f$ on it: we define the linear map
        \begin{equation}
            E_\Sigma : f(x) \mapsto \{f(v_{l+m/2} + w) \mid w \in W_l\}
        \end{equation}
        that evaluates $f(x)$ at $n^\prime < n$ points; however, since the points are in $\mathds{F}_{2^m}$, we
        get $n^\prime \cdot m = n/m\cdot m = n$ values in $\mathds{F}_2$. 

        To perform $E_\Sigma$ we can use the same algorithm used for the LCH FFT, with some modifications:
        recalling that Algorithm~\ref{alg:basiscvt} depends only on the polynomial and that Algorithm~\ref{alg:lchfft} 
        performs the actual butterfly computations, we only have to adapt the latter to the new set of evaluation points.
        In particular, we pretend to evaluate $f$ at $v_{l+m/2} + W_l$, but \emph{truncate} the first $l_m$ layers of
        the butterflies, as we do not need the whole evaluation.
        
        \begin{example}
            Let's consider the calculation of the first value $f_0$ for $m=4$ and a generic $n$. Starting from $4$ points
            $(a_{0\cdot n^\prime}, a_{1\cdot n^\prime}, a_{2\cdot n^\prime}, a_{3\cdot n^\prime})$, each from $\mathds{F}_2$,
            the first $l_m=2$ layers of the butterfly stage are truncated as shown in Figure \ref{fig:trunc-fft}. 
            The multiplications, denoted by ($\bullet$), produce the output:
            \begin{equation*}
                f_0 = a_{0\cdot n^\prime} + s_{l+1}(v_{l+m/2})\cdot a_{1\cdot n^\prime} + s_{l+2}(v_{l+m/2})\cdot a_{2\cdot n^\prime} + s_{l+1}(v_{l+m/2})\cdot s_{l+2}(v_{l+m/2})\cdot a_{3\cdot n^\prime}
            \end{equation*}
            \input{figures/trunc-fft.tex}
            Because of the recursive nature of the vanishing polynomial, we can rewrite the result above as:
            \begin{equation*}
                f_0 = a_{0\cdot n^\prime} + v_{m/2-1}\cdot a_{1\cdot n^\prime} + v_{m/2-2}\cdot a_{2\cdot n^\prime} + v_{m/2-2}\cdot a_{3\cdot n^\prime} 
            \end{equation*}
            where the multipliers are independent of $l$ for a fixed $m$. Since the multipliers are in $\mathds{F}_{2^m}$ the output $f_0$ is in the same field, 
            thus not requiring the computation of the remaining values.
        \end{example}

    After this step, we can finally carry out the remaining butterfly computations, evaluating the encoded polynomials at the points in $\Sigma$.

    We can finally summarize the Frobenius Additive FFT algorithm in Algorithm~\ref{alg:fafft}.

    \begin{algorithm2e}[H]
        \caption{Frobenius Additive FFT Multiplication}
        \label{alg:fafft}
        \KwIn{$A(x) = a_0 + a_1x + \cdots + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} \in \mathds{F}_2[x]_{<n}$,\newline
            $B(x) = b_0 + b_1x + \cdots + b_{n-1}x^{n-1} \in \mathds{F}_2[x]_{<n}$}
        \KwOut{$C(x) = c_0 + c_1x + \cdots + c_{n-1}x^{n-1} = A(x)\cdot B(x) \mod (x^n+1) \in \mathds{F}_2[x]_{<n}$}
        $\bar{A}(X) \gets \textsc{BasisCvt}(A(x)) \in \mathds{F}_2[x]_{<n}$\;
        $\bar{B}(X) \gets \textsc{BasisCvt}(B(x)) \in \mathds{F}_2[x]_{<n}$\;
        $\tilde{A}(X) \gets \textsc{Encode}(\bar{A}(X)) \in \mathds{F}_{2^m}[x]_{<n^\prime}$\;
        $\tilde{B}(X) \gets \textsc{Encode}(\bar{B}(X)) \in \mathds{F}_{2^m}[x]_{<n^\prime}$\;
        $[\hat{a}_1, \cdots, \hat{a}_{n^\prime}] \gets \textsc{Butterflies}(\tilde{A}(X), \Sigma) \in \mathds{F}_{2^m}^{n^\prime}$\;
        $[\hat{b}_1, \cdots, \hat{b}_{n^\prime}] \gets \textsc{Butterflies}(\tilde{B}(X), \Sigma) \in \mathds{F}_{2^m}^{n^\prime}$\;
        $\hat{C} = [\hat{c}_1, \cdots, \hat{c}_{n^\prime}] \gets [\hat{a}_1\cdot \hat{b}_1, \cdots, \hat{a}_{n^\prime}\cdot \hat{b}_{n^\prime}]$\;
        $\tilde{C}(X) \gets \textsc{Butterflies}^{-1}(\hat{C}, \Sigma) \in \mathds{F}_{2^m}[x]_{<n^\prime}$\;
        $\bar{C}(X) \gets \textsc{Decode}(\tilde{C}(X)) \in \mathds{F}_2[x]_{<n}$\;
        $C(x) \gets \textsc{BasisCvt}^{-1}(\bar{C}(X)) \in \mathds{F}_2[x]_{<n}$\;
        \KwRet{$C(x)$}
    \end{algorithm2e}
