Post-quantum cryptography (PQC), which is also known as quantum-resistant 
cryptography, aims at defining new public-key cryptographic schemes based 
on mathematical problems proven to be hard against adversaries equipped with 
both classical and quantum computers. Recent advancements on quantum computer 
technology have witnessed a steep increase in scientific and engineering 
activities to deliver new solutions to some of the most pressing scientific 
and societal challenges~\cite{biondi2021quantum}. However, from a cybersecurity standpoint, 
the availability of a quantum computer with a sufficiently large number of 
qubits, could leave most of the current protocols used to guarantee the 
confidentiality and authenticity of communications and storage of data  
vulnerable to cryptanalytic techniques based on Shor~\cite{DBLP:conf/focs/Shor94}-like quantum algorithms 
that can subvert the security guarantees of the underlying 
public key cryptosystems employing only a polynomial computational effort 
w.r.t. the exponential one required by classical computational platforms.
Since 2017, several institutional and governmental bodies (e.g.,
The Center for Advanced China Research - CACR,    
The European Quantum Communications Infrastructure - EuroQCI - initiative) 
promoted the analysis of quantum-secure systems, but most market stakeholders 
are focused on tracking efforts being pursued in the 
PQC standardization contest promoted by the U.S. National Institute of Standard (NIST).  
Through two rounds of this evaluation process, NIST selected seven finalists
and eight alternate candidates, which were then thinned down to two standard
candidates and four alternates for a final round of cryptanalysis and possible 
standardization. 
The current standardization-elected candidate Key Encapsulation Method (KEM) is 
Kyber~\cite{Kyber} is a lattice-based cryptosystem, while NIST selected four 
candidates relying on different computationally hard problems, in order to obtain
resilience against potentially disruptive attacks on lattice-based cryptosystems.
Among the four candidates, a polynomial-time cryptanalytic attack eliminated
SIKE, leaving only three KEMs which are based based on the hardness of the 
syndrome decoding problem, i.e., Classic McEliece~\cite{ClassicMcEliece}, 
BIKE~\cite{BIKE} and HQC~\cite{melchor2018hamming}. 
Among these candidates, only HQC can exhibit a security proof 
based on a polynomial reduction of the solution of a computationally
hard problem to the breaking of the cryptosystem.
In particular, any successful break of HQC implies a faster way to solve the
syndrome decoding problem on a random quasi-cyclic code of rate $\frac{1}{2}$.
By contrast, Classic McEliece relies also on the indistinguishability 
of a hidden Goppa code from a random code, and BIKE relies on the indistinguishability
of a hidden QC-MDPC code from a random quasi cyclic code.
While both arguments are currently unchallenged, there is no proof that they
are indeed corresponding to computationally hard problems.

Hamming Quasi-Cyclic (HQC)~\cite{melchor2018hamming,DBLP:journals/tit/Aguilar-Melchor18} is
a code-based IND-CCA2 secure KEM whose security relies on the syndrome 
decoding problem for quasi cyclic codes. It is obtained by applying the HHK 
transform~\cite{DBLP:conf/tcc/HofheinzHK17} on a IND-CPA construction, which 
is also denoted as HQC.PKE.
HQC uses two types of codes: a tensor code (obtained concatenating a Reed-Muller 
and Reed-Solomon code) with a public generator matrix $G$ and a random
double-circulant [$2n, n$]-code with a systematic parity check matrix $(1, h)$.
It is worth noting that the public code with generator matrix $G$ is only employed
for its error correcting capability, and plays no role in the security proofs 
(indeed, it is public).

Given the sound security record of HQC, much attention has been devoted to its 
side-channel cryptanalysis.
Most of the independent analyses focused mainly on power analysis or on 
extracting electromagnetic traces ~\cite{wcc22,DBLP:conf/pqcrypto/GoyLG22,DBLP:conf/pqcrypto/SchambergerHRWS22,DBLP:conf/cardis/SchambergerRSW20}, whilst by the end of the $2$nd round of the U.S. NIST contest 
the authors in ~\cite{DBLP:journals/amco/Wafo-TapaBBGM22} 
and~\cite{DBLP:conf/sacrypt/PaivaT19} presented a practical key recovery 
strategy based on the effectiveness of a chosen ciphertext timing attack against 
the non-constant time implementation of the decoder of the 
Bose-Chaudhuri-Hocquenghem (BCH) code (which was employed before the current 
concatenated Reed Mueller-Reed Solomon code). 
Both approaches exploit the dependence between the running time of the decoding 
procedure and the number of decoded errors.  Specifically, when considering the 
HQC instance exhibiting $128$-bit security parameters, the attack 
in~\cite{DBLP:conf/sacrypt/PaivaT19} requires $400$ Mdecryption runs, while 
the one in~\cite{DBLP:journals/amco/Wafo-TapaBBGM22} 
reaches a key recovery after just $5,441$ calls with $93\%$ success rate. 
Both attacks proposed as a solution the design of a constant-time decoding 
algorithm without introducing a significant penalty in terms of computational 
complexity.
Nonetheless, in~\cite{DBLP:journals/tches/GuoHJLNS22} the authors described a 
timing-based key-recovery  attack against HQC based on an implementation 
weakness in the step that used a procedure called {\em rejection sampling} 
during randomness generation. As a countermeasure, a more 
careful implementation of the rejection sampling step is effective to prevent 
the attack.

To date, no dedicated, constant time implementation of HQC targeted to embedded 
systems has been proposed, despite the fact that timing attacks are easier to
carry out against them.
Moreover, we also note that most of the literature focused on side-channel
attacks aimed at key retrieval, while it is also possible to perform a 
message-retrieval attack against the encryption primitive, in case the 
operations in the said encryption are not constant time.
Indeed, the attack mechanism mimics the common attacks on modular exponentiation, 
as the non-constant time polynomial multiplication techniques, such as the one
in the reference code by HQC, have a loop whose body contains branches depending
on the values of one of the operands.
Since, in the HQC.PKE encryption primitive, one of such operands is secret 
(as it is the plaintext to be encrypted), providing constant time multiplication
algorithms is needed to secure the encryption primitive. Furthermore,
as the HHK transform requires a execution of the HQC.PKE encryption function
during the computation of the HQC KEM decryption, the requirement for constant
time multiplication implementations is further strengthened.

The current state of the software HQC implementations tailored to the ARM
Cortex-M$4$ microcontroller (chosen by NIST as the reference platform for
microcontroller benchmarks) includes only the one in PQM$4$~\cite{PQM4}
a public software project which collects the implementation of all NIST
fourth round candidates tailored for the Cortex M$4$ CPUs.
In the PQM4 project, which is currently being used by NIST as one of the third
party benchmarking suites, the latest version of the HQC implementation is directly
derived from the third round reference implementation, 
without further tailored optimizations.
As a consequence, the implementation is vulnerable to the timing side-channel 
attack by~\cite{DBLP:journals/tches/GuoHJLNS22}.


\noindent\textbf{Our Contribution.} In this paper, we present the first fully 
constant-time software implementation of HQC, together with a comparative analysis
of constant time binary polynomial multiplication techniques, tailored to Cortex-M$4$ 
microcontrollers. 
We provide an experimental validation of the constant time property via a battery
of statistical $t$-tests, while varying the CPU frequency and memory latencies, 
to highlight the stability of our solution across different memory access 
latencies (which depend on the CPU frequency). 
Our results show that employing a FFT based multiplication algorithm is the 
optimal choice for constant-time multiplications, in contrast with classic
divide-et-impera techniques (e.g., Karatsuba) which provide a better tradeoff
for x$86$\_$64$ computing platforms.
